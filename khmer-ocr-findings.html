Building a Khmer Vision OCR with Structured Layout Output

A grounded assessment of your plan — fine-tuning DeepSeek-OCR-2, building a Khmer dataset, and going font-agnostic — plus the one reframe that will save you the most time.

Prepared 27 May 2026 · Base model: deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-OCR-2 · Sources linked throughout and listed at the end.

Short answer: yes, this is very achievable — and you've picked the right base model.

DeepSeek-OCR-2 is genuinely well-suited to what you want: it already does grounding mode with layout, markdown, and tables, it's multilingual, Apache-2.0, and has first-class fine-tuning support. A directly comparable case — fine-tuning DeepSeek-OCR on a 200K Persian dataset — cut Character Error Rate by ~88% (149% → 61%) in just 60 training steps. Khmer is a harder script than Persian, but the recipe transfers.

The one thing to change in your plan: don't think of it as "scrape an OCR dataset." Scraping gives you text, not image-text pairs. The winning move for every low-resource OCR effort (Arabic SARD, NVIDIA Nemotron, SynthOCR-Gen) is scrape a clean text corpus, then render it synthetically into document images. That gives you perfect, free ground truth — including layout boxes — and makes font-agnostic training automatic.

1DeepSeek-OCR-2 as your base

DeepSeek-OCR-2 (released 27 Jan 2026) is a 3B-parameter vision-language model. Its predecessor introduced "contexts optical compression" — rendering text to images and encoding them into very few vision tokens — and v2 adds DeepEncoder V2 with a "visual causal flow" / human-like reading order, which is exactly what you want for layout-aware reading.

2Reframing the dataset strategy

Your instinct to build the dataset yourself is correct — Khmer is low-resource and you'll need data nobody else has. But the framing matters enormously:

❌ "Scrape an OCR dataset"

Scraping Khmer news yields HTML text. To turn that into OCR training data you'd have to find or photograph matching images and hand-label them — slow, expensive, and you can't control layout.

✅ "Scrape a corpus, render it"

Scraping yields a clean text corpus. You then render it into document images with a layout engine. Ground truth (text + boxes + structure) is generated for free and perfectly.

This synthetic-first recipe is how every recent low-resource and multilingual OCR system was built: SynthOCR-Gen is a whole generator for exactly this (Unicode normalization, script-purity enforcement, multi-font rendering, 25+ degradation augmentations); NVIDIA Nemotron OCR v2 trained on 12M synthetic images across six languages; the SARD Arabic dataset rendered 843K book-style pages (690M words) across 10 fonts. Synthetic data is what makes the model font- and layout-invariant.

The payoff Because you control the renderer, every synthetic page comes with exact bounding boxes and structure tags — so you can train DeepSeek-OCR-2's grounding mode directly. No manual box-drawing. Scraping still matters, but its job is text diversity, not images.

3Khmer-specific traps that will silently wreck your labels

Khmer is one of the harder scripts for OCR — large character set, stacked/subscript consonants (coeng ◌្), vowels that attach above/below/around the base, complex syllable clusters, and no spaces between words. These create two failure modes most people miss:

Trap A — Unicode normalization & ordering

The same visible Khmer syllable can be encoded with characters in different orders. If your corpus contains malformed or inconsistently ordered sequences, your "ground truth" labels are wrong before training even starts — and the model learns garbage. You must run Khmer canonical normalization / script-purity enforcement on every scraped string (drop non-Khmer noise, fix coeng+vowel ordering, NFC). This is the single highest-leverage data-quality step.

Trap B — Text shaping at render time

Khmer needs complex text layout (glyph reordering + ligatures) to render correctly. Naïve renderers (e.g. plain Pillow ImageDraw.text) do not shape Khmer — subscripts land in the wrong place and your image won't match your label. You must render through a HarfBuzz-based shaper: Pillow built with libraqm, or Pango/Cairo, or an HTML→image path (Playwright / wkhtmltoimage) which uses the browser's shaper.

Build this guard first Render a sample, OCR-free, then round-trip check: re-extract text from your rendered image's source string and diff. Better: visually QA a sample grid across all fonts. A shaping bug produces images that look "almost right" but teach the model wrong glyph order — and you won't catch it in the loss curve, only in real-world failure.

Trap C — Word boxes are ambiguous

With no inter-word spaces, "word-level" bounding boxes aren't well-defined in Khmer. Decide your granularity up front: line-level boxes (recommended, robust) or grapheme-cluster boxes. Don't promise word boxes you can't reliably generate. (If you ever need word segmentation, khPOS and CRF segmenters exist — see §9.)

4Font-agnostic, done right

Your font-swapping idea is exactly right — it's the primary lever for generalization. Research on 19 Khmer fonts with Tesseract shows accuracy swings wildly by font:

✅ Easy fonts

Battambang, Khmer OS, Siemreap, Odor MeanChey, Sithi Manuss — CER ~0.01.

⚠️ Hard fonts

Dangrek (CER 0.29), iSeth First (0.23), Bayon (0.21) — decorative / unusual shaping.

Practical rules for the render set:

  • Deliberately over-sample the hard fonts. They're where OCR fails, so they're where training signal is most valuable. Don't just train on the pretty ones.
  • Validate glyph coverage per font. Many Khmer fonts don't support every subscript/diacritic and will render .notdef boxes (□). Programmatically check each font covers your character set before including it; otherwise you inject more bad labels.
  • Randomize aggressively: font, size, weight, line spacing, color, background, slight rotation/skew, blur, JPEG artifacts, paper texture, lighting — this "domain randomization" is what closes the synthetic→real gap.
  • Mix in handwriting later. Printed first; if you need photos/handwritten, the historical Sleuk Rith palm-leaf data and the KhmerST scene-text benchmark exist as references.

5Defining "structured layout output"

This is a design decision to lock down on day one, because it determines your label format. The strongest path is to emit in DeepSeek-OCR-2's native grounding format so fine-tuning teaches Khmer recognition, not a new output schema. Decide what structure you actually need:

TargetOutput shapeUse when
Reading-order textPlain UTF-8, correct orderSearch, TTS, translation feed
MarkdownHeadings, lists, tables, boldDocument reconstruction / RAG
Grounded layoutBlocks + bounding boxes + type tagYou need where + what (forms, invoices, IDs)
JSON treeNested blocks: title/para/table/figureDownstream programmatic consumption
Recommendation Generate ground truth as grounded markdown (DeepSeek's <|grounding|> format: markdown text interleaved with normalized bounding boxes + block types). It covers reading order, tables, and spatial layout in one representation, and matches the model's pre-training so fine-tuning converges fast. If a consumer needs JSON, post-process the markdown+boxes — don't make the model learn two formats.

7Phased roadmap

0
Week 1 · Foundations

Define output schema + build a real eval set

Lock the grounded-markdown format. Collect 200–500 real Khmer pages (news scans, photos, PDFs) and hand-correct them. This is your North Star — without a real held-out set you cannot tell if you're improving. Run DeepSeek-OCR-2 zero-shot on it to record the baseline CER/WER/TEDS.

1
Weeks 2–3 · Corpus

Collect & clean the text corpus

Bootstrap from existing open Khmer corpora (§9), then scrape news/blogs for domain and vocabulary breadth. Normalize (script purity, NFC, coeng ordering), dedup, and filter. Respect robots.txt, rate-limit, and prefer permissively-licensed sources (see §10).

2
Weeks 3–5 · Synthesis

Build the rendering pipeline

HarfBuzz-shaped, multi-font rendering with layout templates and auto-generated boxes. Start with ~50–100K pages; scale to 500K+ once quality is verified. Reuse or fork SynthOCR-Gen rather than building from zero. QA a visual grid across all fonts before mass-generating.

3
Weeks 5–6 · First training

LoRA fine-tune via Unsloth, then iterate

Fine-tune on synthetic data; evaluate against your real set every run. Expect a large CER drop early (cf. Persian: −88% in 60 steps). Diagnose failures by font/layout and feed the renderer to cover the gaps. Tight loop: train → eval → analyze → adjust synthesis.

4
Weeks 7+ · Close the gap

Add real labeled data & harden

Mix a few thousand real, hand-corrected pages into training to close the synthetic→real gap. Add hard cases: low-quality scans, mixed Khmer/English, tables, ID cards. Consider full fine-tune (not just LoRA) once data is mature. Serve via vLLM.

10Risks & pitfalls to plan around

  • Scraping legality. Khmer news content is copyrighted. For a text corpus used to train a model this is usually lower-risk, but respect robots.txt, rate-limit, don't redistribute raw articles, and prefer CC-licensed sources (Wikipedia, OSCAR) where possible. If commercializing, get counsel.
  • Silent shaping/normalization bugs. The deadliest risk (§3): wrong labels that look fine. Build round-trip + visual QA gates before mass generation.
  • Synthetic→real gap. A model that's perfect on synthetic pages can fail on real photos. Domain randomization + a real fine-tune slice are the fix; your real eval set is how you detect it.
  • Tokenizer fertility on Khmer. Measure it early; poor coverage inflates sequence length and cost.
  • Over-fitting to easy fonts. Balance the render mix toward the hard fonts.
  • Layout label ambiguity. Commit to line-level boxes; don't over-promise word boxes in a space-less script.
  • Eval honesty. Keep the real eval set strictly held out — never let synthetic versions of it leak into training.
